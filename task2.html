Task 2 · Design a feature from customer feedback

Zanko makes the reports. It cannot send them.

Three customers, three roles, the same gap: Zanko already makes the report each one needs, but a person still carries it to its readers — by logging in, copying, exporting, trimming, and forwarding. The feature is Scheduled Reports: Zanko sends the reports — on a schedule, to the right people, in the version each person needs.

TL;DR — 30 seconds

  • The problem: Zanko makes the reports but cannot send them. The Reporting page is a list of reports with no send button — a report waits there until someone logs in and fetches it. So the people became the delivery system. Every quote describes delivery work done by hand.
  • The feature: a Schedules tab in Reporting. Each row is one schedule, and a schedule is four settings: which report, when it goes, who gets it, and which of two versions each person gets. Set it once and it runs until paused.
  • Trust: an email to someone outside the company waits for a person to click Approve. Inside the company, it sends on its own.
  • Version two, on purpose: auto-send for outside email (after the approve click has earned trust), picking your own sections (version one has two fixed versions), and loud failure handling — a red row with one-click retry (version one records every send).
  • Proof: the clickable prototype — the Schedules list, the schedule screen with a live email preview, the approval gate, and the phone-sized executive email.

1 · What the three customers said

Customer 1 A compliance manager at a mid-size lender. Every Monday she logs in, copies the complaint summary into an email, and sends it to her sponsor bank. Her words: "It eats the first hour of my week."

The detail that matters: she asks for the report "ready for me" — not "sent for me." She wants the work done, but the send kept under her control. Her reader is outside the company, and her company answers to it.

Customer 2 An operations lead at a fintech. He exports one report, trims a short copy for some readers, and forwards both around. His words: "I'd love to decide once who gets what, and then stop thinking about it."

The detail that matters: his two audiences, in his own words, want "the detailed breakdown" and "the headline numbers." Two versions of one report — not more reports.

Customer 3 A VP of Customer Experience. She never logs into the tool. She wants a monthly summary she can read on her phone before a board meeting. Her words: "are we getting better or worse."

The detail that matters: she named where she reads: her phone, by email, no login. For her, the email is the product.

The pattern: Zanko makes the reports. Zanko cannot send them. The proof is on the product's own Reporting page — it is a list of reports with no send button. A report waits in that list until someone logs in and fetches it. That is why all three customers became the delivery system themselves: the manual work in every quote is delivery work. The feature is scheduled delivery — Zanko sends the reports.

One assumption, stated: scheduling a report treats it as something Zanko can re-run each period, not a one-time snapshot. The example report ("Generated by Ask Zanko") makes that a safe bet. If a customer's Monday rundown is one-off today, any generated report gets a "Make this recurring" action that opens the same schedule screen.

Who it's for: the people who set it up are the compliance manager and the operations lead — existing Zanko users who owe someone a report. The people it serves include people who will never log in: the VP, and the sponsor bank. The feature is judged by how little the readers have to do — nothing.

3 · What's in version one — and what is not

In

  • A Schedules tab in Reporting. Each row is one schedule — name, source report, recipients, when it goes, and the next run — active until paused. The prototype shows three schedules, one for each customer: the Monday email to the sponsor bank, the ops team's weekly detail, and the executive's monthly summary. R5 R8
  • The schedule screen — four settings: which report, when it goes, who gets it, and which of two versions each person gets. A live preview of the email sits beside the four settings the whole time, so you see what each reader gets before you commit. R1 R3 R7
  • Two versions, in the customers' own words: the detailed one is the full report; the headline one is the report's own top section — the Executive Summary the example report already opens with. R3 R4
  • The approve click: when a reader is outside the company, the email waits for the owner to review it and click Approve & send. Inside the company, it sends on its own. The compliance manager's Monday hour becomes one look and one click. R7
  • Send history per schedule — who got which version, when, and whether it was delivered. The answer to "did the bank get it?" R6
  • Pause, don't delete: pausing keeps the history. It is an audit record, not just a log. R5 R6

What this feature is not

  • It is not a report builder. Not in version two either. The brief shows the reports already exist ("Generated by Ask Zanko") and says not to design report contents. This feature only delivers them.
  • No auto-send for outside email until the approve click has earned trust. That is version two.
  • No picking your own sections. Version one has two versions: the full report, and the report's own top section. The section picker is version two.
  • No Slack and no texts. Nobody in the quotes asked for them. Email is what the VP asked to read.

4 · Design decisions

The biggest decisions

1 — Delivery, not report design. Zanko already makes the reports. The feature only sends them. That keeps version one small, honest to the quotes, and scalable — anything the Reports tab can make, the Schedules tab can deliver.

2 — The version belongs to the person, not the report. One schedule, one report, and each reader tagged with the version they get. The operations lead decides once; nobody maintains three diverging copies.

3 — Trust is a feature: the approve click, the send record, and loud failure. An automatic email to a sponsor bank must be reviewable before it leaves and provable after it does. Same rule as Task 1: nothing leaves the company without a person.

Information design

DecisionWhy (traced)
Schedules is a third tab beside Dashboards and Reports, reusing the existing list layout, search, and row style of the Reports tab. It reads as the Reporting area growing, not a bolt-on. R8
The list stays calm — every row reads name, report, recipients, when it goes, and the next run, with a quiet Active / Paused state. When a send is waiting for a person, that one row surfaces a Review & approve button. The owner's Monday question is "does anything need me?" — a quiet list means no, and the one row that does need her says so. R5 R7
The live preview is half the schedule screen, switchable between the full report, the headline version, and a phone frame. You are deciding what people receive; you should be looking at it while you decide. R3 R4
The headline email leads with a verdict line ("Overall: improving…") above four big numbers. In version one the verdict is computed from which way the numbers moved — simple up-or-down math, not free-form AI prose — so nothing mailed to a VP or a bank can make things up. The VP's literal question is "are we getting better or worse" — so the email answers it in its first line. R4

Behavioral design

DecisionWhy (traced)
An outside reader turns "review before send" on by default. The owner can turn it off, but the safe path is the default path. An unapproved email holds and asks again — nothing outside the company ever sends itself on a timer. (Yes, this keeps one weekly click the operations lead wanted to lose — deliberate friction, only where the reader is regulated.) A sponsor bank email is a compliance record; the default carries the judgment. R7
Outside emails carry the summary plus an expiring secure link — complaint-level data never rides in the email body. Inside readers get the numbers in the email itself, since "read on my phone without logging in" is the VP's whole use case. Emailing regulated data outside the company deserves a colder default than emailing your own VP. R2 R7
Approving is one click from the list — review the rendered email, Approve & send. Editing reopens the schedule, not the report. The compliance manager's hour becomes one look and one click. R7 R2
Failures notify the owner and stay red in the list until retried or resolved. A silent failure to a regulator-adjacent audience is the worst outcome this feature can have. R9
Every send writes a history row (version, recipients, timestamp, delivery status), kept even if the schedule is paused or deleted. "Did they get it?" must have a provable answer. R6

Feasibility note. This leans on one thing Zanko demonstrably has — report generation ("Generated by Ask Zanko" on the example report) — plus two new building blocks: sending email, and a scheduler with an approval queue. The headline version uses the same data as the full report (the big numbers, their movement, the top themes), and its verdict line is that same up-or-down math, so version one needs no new analytics or AI output. A schedule includes day and time (the Monday report must land before Monday morning), and each run covers the period since the last.

How I'd know it worked

Three signals: manual exports drop (assuming export events are logged); schedules survive (created-then-paused-forever means the setting didn't fit the need); and approval speed (if the owner approves within minutes every Monday, the approve click is working — if approvals pile up, the default needs tuning).
