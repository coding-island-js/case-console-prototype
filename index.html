Task 1 — Case View redesign

The problem

A complaints analyst lives in this screen for around six hours a day, and the brief says she should always know two things: where the case is in its workflow, and what to do next. The current screen answers neither. It shows every field the database has, in database order — but the complaint itself is cut off behind a "See More" click, the AI's conclusions appear as bare scores with no explanation and no way to disagree, her main deliverable (the draft response) is hidden in a tab below the fold, and nothing anywhere shows the workflow. The screen even shows dates that read as contradictory — a ticket closed before it was resolved, on a case still waiting on the customer — and gives the analyst no way to tell whether that's an error or just unexplained. In short: it renders the database record, not the analyst's job.

What I built

Minimum intervention: Zanko's existing screen — same sections, same labels, same right rail — plus five fixes, each mapped to a diagnosed problem. One added bar shows the workflow steps and one suggested next action. The complaint shows in full. The existing AI fields now say how sure they are, offer their reasoning one click away, and take Agree or Disagree with a reason that's saved for auditors — and the reply cannot be sent until a human has judged the analysis (Zanko's own site: "human-in-the-loop review option before sending"). The draft reply opens itself when it's time to respond, and one verb per button: send sends, resolve resolves. Values that haven't happened yet show a dash until they're real. Analysts relearn nothing.

Task 2 — What should Zanko Reporting do next?

The problem

Three customers, three roles, one complaint said three ways: the reports live inside Zanko, but the people who need them don't. A compliance manager rebuilds the same summary by hand every Monday and emails it to her sponsor bank — it costs her the first hour of every week. An operations lead exports one report and trims it into different versions for each audience, over and over. And a VP wants a clean monthly summary on her phone before board meetings — she's never going to log in, and no feature should require her to. Zanko already makes all of these reports — but it cannot send them. The Reporting page is a list with no send button, so the people became the delivery system.

What I built

Scheduled Reports — a Schedules tab inside the existing Reporting area. Pick which report Zanko already generates, when it goes, and who gets it, where each recipient gets one of two versions: the full report, or the headline — the report's own top section, phone friendly, opening with the answer to "are we getting better or worse." It delivers by email and runs until paused. Because a sponsor bank is a regulated audience, external sends wait for a one-click Approve & send — the compliance manager's Monday hour becomes a minute without giving up control. Every send is recorded (who got which version, when, delivered or not). Version two, on purpose: auto-send for outside email (after the approve click has earned trust), picking your own sections (version one has two fixed versions), and loud failure handling — a red row with one-click retry. And it is not a report designer — Zanko already makes the reports; this feature only delivers them.

AI tools used & time spent

Tools: Claude Code (Anthropic) end to end — reading the brief, drafting both diagnoses, and building this site and the prototypes in plain HTML/CSS/JS. Before finalizing each task, an independent AI review pass attacked my own framing; it caught one misreading of the current screen in Task 1 and several soft spots in Task 2 (both corrected) — the same check I'd run on my own work regardless of tooling. No frameworks, no build step; hosted on Netlify, auto-deployed from GitHub.

My role vs. the AI's: the problem framing, the requirements, the scoping calls, and every design decision were mine to direct and approve; the AI accelerated the reading, the writing, and the construction.

One thing this exercise sharpened for me: AI assistance moves the scope line. Mechanical completeness — states, edge cases, keyboard polish — has become cheap, so it stays in a v1. What still costs real time is judgment: features that need requirements interrogated and designs iterated by a person. Those are what a first version should cut, and that's how I scoped both tasks here.

Time: about 6 hours total across the two tasks, within the brief's 4–6 hour target.
