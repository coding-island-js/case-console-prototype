Task 1 · Diagnose & Redesign

The case view renders the record, not the job

Diagnosis of the current Case (Complaint) view, and the reasoning behind the redesign — personas → scenario → requirements → design, so every decision is traceable to a moment in the analyst's day.

TL;DR — 30 seconds

  • Diagnosis: the screen renders the database record, not the analyst's job — no workflow position, no next action, AI conclusions shown as bare scores she can neither question nor correct. It even shows state that reads as contradictory (closed before resolved, on a case still "waiting on customer") with nothing to reconcile it.
  • Redesign: minimum intervention — Zanko's existing screen, unchanged, plus five fixes: an added steps-and-next-action bar; the complaint shown in full; confidence, an expandable "why," and Agree/Disagree woven into the existing analysis fields; the draft reply surfaced when it's time to respond; resolution fields only on resolved cases. Analysts relearn nothing.
  • Left out on purpose: queue redesign, nav changes, AI configuration, layout customization, real backend.
  • Proof: the clickable prototype — six states, working actions, in Zanko's own design language. Everything below is the reasoning.

1 · Who this screen is for

The brief defines the user; these personas make her goals concrete enough to design against. The analyst, the 6 hours/day, and the review → investigate → respond → resolve workflow come from the exercise document; the queue sizes, the SLA framing, and Dana are stated working assumptions — reasonable for regulated complaint operations, and cheap to correct with one customer conversation.

Maya — Complaints Analyst primary

Works a queue of 30–50 open cases, ~6 hours a day in this screen. Expert user by week two — she needs scan speed, not onboarding.

  • Goals: clear the queue without mistakes; hit every SLA date (the promised response deadline); never get burned by trusting a wrong AI conclusion; be defensible when a case is audited.
  • Measured on: throughput, resolution quality, SLA compliance.
  • Reality: the AI is usually right — so the dangerous moments are the cases where it isn't, and the screen must make those stand out.

Dana — Compliance Manager secondary

Doesn't work cases day-to-day; audits them after the fact and answers to regulators and the sponsor bank.

  • Goals: reconstruct any case — who did what, when, and why, including every point where a human agreed with or overrode the AI.
  • Implication: the audit trail and AI-decision history can't be buried; they're a first-class part of the record.

2 · Maya's day, one case at a time

Before drawing anything, here is the sequence Maya runs dozens of times a day. Each moment gets an ID so requirements and design elements can cite it.

  1. S1 Orient. She opens a case from her queue and needs to know in seconds: who is this, what product, how risky, and when is it due.
  2. S2 Read. She reads the complaint itself — sometimes two sentences, sometimes two pages of forwarded email.
  3. S3 Judge the AI. She reviews what Zanko concluded (summary, category, risk) and decides whether to trust it — which requires knowing why it concluded that and how confident it is.
  4. S4 Locate. She confirms where the case sits in its workflow (review → investigate → respond → resolve) and what the next action is.
  5. S5 Do the stage's work. In Respond, that means reviewing and approving the drafted response; in Investigate, checking the pulled records.
  6. S6 Record. She leaves a comment or a decision note — and everything she and the AI did must land in the audit trail.
  7. S7 Advance. She moves the case to its next stage and returns to the queue. Repeat ~40 times a day — seconds saved here compound.
  8. S8 Audit (Dana, weeks later). Every judgment in S3–S7 must be reconstructable, especially human overrides of the AI.

4 · Diagnosis of the current screen

What already works

Credit where due: the left nav is clean and stays out of the way; grouping metadata into a right rail is the correct instinct; "Ask Zanko" is one click from anywhere; and the visual language (calm cards, restrained color) is right for a tool used six hours a day. The redesign keeps all of this. The problems are structural, not cosmetic.

Why it's probably like this — and why that's now the problem

To be fair to the screen: it reads as a generic record-detail template — field rows, likely generated or assembled quickly — and that was probably the right call to get from 4 design partners to 30+ clients fast. The "Sunsetting" and "Beta" badges in the nav suggest a platform mid-migration. But the analyst's volume has outgrown the template: at six hours a day, its costs now compound on every case.

Root cause

The screen renders the database record, not the analyst's job. Every field gets an identical flat row in database order, so the layout answers "what columns does a complaint have?" instead of Maya's four questions: what is this case (S1), can I trust the AI (S3), where is it in the workflow (S4), and what do I do now (S7)? Three deeper patterns follow from it:

  1. The workflow doesn't exist on screen. Nothing shows review → investigate → respond → resolve, and a "Waiting on customer" status dropdown is not a workflow position. Violates R5 R6 — the two things the brief says Maya must always know.
  2. Layout follows the database, not the analyst's reading order. The complaint and the AI summary — the two things she must actually read — are truncated behind "See More", while single dates get full-width rows. Violates R2 R9.
  3. AI output is dumped as fields, not presented as an assessment. "Resolution Quality Score: 50" with no why, no confidence, no override. She's asked to trust or question the AI but given no means to do either. Violates R3 R4.

5 · The redesign, decision by decision

The three decisions that matter most

1 — Minimum intervention. The current screen's layout survives untouched; every change maps to a diagnosed problem. A redesign nobody has to relearn is a redesign that can actually ship.

2 — The workflow became visible, with one computed next action. One added bar answers the brief's two "always know" demands; the button's label defines each stage concretely.

3 — AI conclusions became accountable. Each AI field shows how sure it is; the "why" is one click away; Agree/Disagree records the human's call — the brief's "trust (or question) the AI," made into buttons.

The design philosophy is minimum intervention: their screen, their sections, their labels, their right rail — untouched. Five changes only, each traceable to the diagnosis:

ChangeFixes
1 · One added bar — workflow steps, the due date, and a single next-action button whose label names the stage's exit ("Start investigating" → "Write the reply" → "Approve & send reply" → "Mark resolved"). No workflow position, no next action anywhere R1 R5 R6 R10
2 · The complaint shows in full. Genuinely long threads fold under the reader's control. Reading the case cost a "See More" click on every case R2
3 · The existing Zanko Analysis fields carry confidence, an expandable "why" with quoted evidence, and Agree / Disagree with a required, recorded reason. When confidence is low, the reasoning opens itself and the reply is held. Bare scores with no reasons and no way to disagree R3 R4
4 · The Draft Response tab opens itself at the Respond stage, the reply framed as a letter with Approve & send inside. Her main work product was hidden behind an unselected tab R6 R7
5 · Resolution fields render only on resolved cases, and an overdue case gets the screen's only red banner. Resolution content on open cases; no urgency signal R9 R10

What I removed, and why

  • "Complaint Summary" as a separate field — it restated the description; the assessment's summary now serves that purpose once. R9
  • The "Additional Details" card — its two fields (Agent ID, Customer Email) merged into the single Details card; grouping now follows meaning, not database history. R9
  • Resolution dates on unresolved cases — resolution fields appear when the case reaches Resolve, not before, so the case's state can never look self-contradictory the way the current view's does. R9
  • The generic "Actions ▾" dropdown — replaced by the computed primary action + explicit secondary buttons. R6

What I deliberately did not build

  • No queue/list redesign, no navigation changes — the task is this screen; the nav is kept verbatim so the redesign reads in context.
  • No AI prompt editing or model configuration — trust controls (the why, the confidence, the override) solve Maya's problem without opening a configuration surface.
  • No layout customization / movable panels — one opinionated reading order, consistent across thousands of cases, beats per-user drift in a regulated tool.
  • No real backend — hardcoded data; the judgment being tested lives in structure and behavior, not plumbing.
  • No loading / empty / error states, no keyboard shortcuts — real for production, not where this exercise's judgment lives; both are listed so the cut is a decision, not an oversight.
  • No contextual "Ask Zanko" prompts inside the case — promising (e.g., "ask about this customer's history"), but it's a second feature; the assistant stays where the product already puts it.
